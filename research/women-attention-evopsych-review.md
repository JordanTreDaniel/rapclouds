# "Women are biologically programmed to crave attention" — Scientific Literature Review

Research task for Jordan. All citations below were located and verified via web search / extraction; nothing is invented. Where a source is secondary (e.g., a press release or review article), that is noted.

---

## 1. What evolutionary psychology actually says

Evolutionary psychology (EP) does **not** contain a formal theory or claim that "women are programmed to crave attention." What it contains are narrower claims about **mating psychology**:

- **Trivers (1972), parental investment theory** — The sex that invests more in offspring (in humans, females, via gestation/lactation) evolves to be **more selective/choosy** in mate choice; the less-investing sex (males) competes intrasexually for access. Where male investment strongly exceeds female investment, Trivers explicitly predicted females would compete for males. Source: Trivers 1972, *American Naturalist* (full PDF verified); Wikipedia "Parental investment."
- **Buss (1989)** — "Sex differences in human mate preferences: Evolutionary hypotheses tested in 37 cultures," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 12(1), 1–14 (~7,350 citations). Women cross-culturally desired slightly older mates and prioritized financial prospects/status/ambition (resource differences near-universal: ~97% of cultures for financial prospects, ~78% for ambition; per Brunel review PDF). Physical attractiveness increased desirability of **both** sexes.
- **Buss et al. (1992), sexual jealousy** — *Psychological Science* 3, 251–255. Men were more distressed by a partner's **sexual** infidelity; women were more distressed by a partner's **emotional** infidelity (32% more men than women in the sexual condition; most women in the emotional condition). Physiological measures (EDA, pulse, brow EMG) moved in the predicted directions. Note: this predicts women want **committed mate attention**, not attention in general.
- **Status/prestige, not "attention"** — Cheng et al. (PNAS 2022, "When fertile, women seek status via prestige but not dominance," doi:10.1073/pnas.2205451119; Blake 2022 replication reported in an honors thesis PDF) find men seek status via **dominance** more, women via **prestige** (being admired/respected for skill) — especially during the fertile window. Both sexes use both routes (Cheng et al., 2013, *Current Opinion in Psychology* 2020 review, verified PDF). So EP's account is that women seek **admiration/prestige**, not generic attention.
- **Self-presentation goals** — HBS working paper 16-030 (verified PDF): women listed fewer power-related goals than men (d ≈ 0.34–0.42) and more avoidance goals. Consistent with men being more status/power-seeking.
- **Intrasexual competition in women** — Cambridge Handbook of Evolutionary Perspectives on Sexual Psychology, "Female Intrasexual Competition" chapter (verified): women compete for mates largely via **physical attractiveness enhancement** and indirect aggression. Supporting studies found: Fisher et al. 2004 (*Evolution and Human Behavior*, PubMed 15503995) — female intrasexual competition occurs when high-quality males are treated as a resource; Wang et al. 2021 (*Social Psychological and Personality Science* 12(5), 648–657) — women's intrasexual competition produces beautification; Arnocky et al. 2022 — resource scarcity predicts women's intrasexual competition via envy.
- **Sociometer theory** — Leary et al. 1995; Baumeister, Campbell, Krueger & Vohs 2003 (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 4, 1–44, verified): self-esteem tracks social acceptance for **both** sexes; this is a domain-general mechanism, not a female-specific "attention craving."
- **Objectification theory** — Fredrickson & Roberts 1997 (verified via APA CE material and Moradi & Huang 2008 review): women in sexually objectifying cultures are socialized to adopt an observer's perspective on their own bodies (self-objectification), producing appearance monitoring and appearance-based self-worth. This is a **sociocultural** mechanism, explicitly not an evolved "attention drive."

## 2. Evidence that SUPPORTS a sex difference in attention/social-evaluation seeking

- **Selfie posting volume** — Women post more selfies than men: Sedgewick, Flath & Elias 2017 (*Frontiers in Psychology*, PMC5399073, verified) — on Tinder, 90% of women vs. 54% of men used selfies in profiles; Sorokowska et al. (cited therein) — women post more selfies; a Swansea University press release (2023, secondary) reports ~5 selfies/month for women vs. ~2 for men in a sample.
- **Selfie signaling style** — Sedgewick et al. 2017: men's Tinder selfies angled from **below** (signal power/height); women's from **above** (signal lower power) — consistent with women tailoring appearance signaling to **mate-attraction** norms, not generic attention.
- **Social media engagement** — Pew Research (2015, via Wikipedia "Sex differences in social media use"): 80% of online women vs. 73% of online men use SNSs; women more likely to post photos and maintain current, attractive profile photos (Kolek & Saunders; Strano, as cited). Women use SNSs more explicitly to foster social connections.
- **Appearance-based social evaluation** — Meier & Gray (2014, "Facebook Photo Activity Associated with Body Image Disturbance in Adolescent Girls," verified PDF) — appearance-related Facebook exposure correlated with self-objectification in adolescent girls. Gonzales & Hancock 2011 (*CyberPsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking*, PubMed 21329447, verified) — viewing one's own Facebook profile **enhanced** self-esteem, but the study's gender comparison is uninterpretable (17 women, 4 men) and men actually reported **higher** self-esteem after viewing their profile.
- **Empathy/interpersonal sensitivity** — Christov-Moore et al. (UCLA, ~2014, verified via UCLA Newsroom interview) — females showed greater mirror-pain-region activation when watching others in pain. Behavioral self-report measures generally show higher affective empathy in women (PMC5110041 review, verified).
- **Breakup distress (acute)** — Morris et al. 2021, *Evolutionary Behavioral Sciences* (via Binghamton press release, secondary) — women reported more acute emotional/physical pain after breakups. However, this cuts both ways (see §3).
- **Jealousy over partner's emotional involvement** — Buss et al. 1992 (above); Sagarin et al. 2012 meta-analysis (*Evolution and Human Behavior*, 209 effect sizes, 47 samples, verified) — small-to-moderate theory-supportive sex effects: women relatively more upset by emotional infidelity, men by sexual infidelity.

## 3. Evidence that CONTRADICTS or COMPLICATES the claim

- **Men are not attention-neutral** — Sorokowski et al. 2015 (*Personality and Individual Differences* 85, 123–127, verified via ScienceDirect + PMC7244909): the narcissism–selfie-posting link is **stronger among men than women**. Men also seek status/power goals more (HBS 16-030) and are more sensitive to status comparison: Ratliff & Oishi 2013 (*JPSP*, verified via APA) — men's implicit self-esteem dropped when their female partner succeeded; women's did not shift based on male partners' success. Grandiose/exhibitionist narcissism is more male-coded; women's narcissism, when present, tends toward vulnerable/"Echo" presentation (PMC9578082, verified).
- **Rejection sensitivity is not clearly female** — Downey & Feldman 1996 (*JPSP*, verified via Columbia PDF): "women and men did not differ significantly on mean rejection sensitivity." Zimmer-Gembeck et al. 2016: gender did not moderate rejection sensitivity–depression links. Gender-based rejection sensitivity exists as a construct for women, but general rejection sensitivity is not a female-specific trait.
- **Social pain/ostracism** — Onoda et al. 2010 (Ovid, verified): "Social status, not gender alone, is implicated in different reactions by women and men to social ostracism." The ostracism literature (Williams' Cyberball paradigm; Eisenberger's social pain overlap work) establishes that exclusion hurts everyone via pain-network overlap (dACC/insula), not that women hurt more.
- **Empathy neural meta-analysis** — Lamm, Decety & Singer 2011 (cited in Rueckert 2011 and Pang 2023, verified): fMRI meta-analysis found **no** overall sex difference in neural responses to others' pain. Cultural/socialization effects are strongest on explicit self-report measures (Eisenberg & Lennon 1983; Gleichgerrcht & Decety 2013, per PMC5110041).
- **Breakup recovery** — Helgeson 1994 (*Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 20(3), verified): breakups increased men's distress but **decreased** women's; women adjusted better to separation and breakup. The Binghamton finding (acute pain higher in women) coexists with worse long-term adjustment in men — the "women need partner attention more" reading is not supported.
- **Overall effect sizes are small** — Hyde 2005 (*American Psychologist* 60, 658–681, verified PDF): of 124 effect sizes from 46 meta-analyses, 30% were close-to-zero (d ≤ 0.10) and 48% small (0.11–0.35); 78% were d ≤ 0.35. Zell, Krizan & Teeter 2014 (*American Psychologist*, verified PDF): 386 meta-analytic effects, average absolute d = 0.21. Any "women crave attention" claim is extrapolating from small average differences to a categorical folk-psychology trait.
- **Cultural variation narrows sex differences** — Eagly & Wood 1999 ("The origins of sex differences in human behavior," verified PDF) / social role theory: sex differences in mate preferences shrink as national gender equality rises. Zentner & Mitura 2012 (*Psychological Science* 23, 1176–1185, verified via JSTOR/Semantic Scholar): nations' gender gap predicts degree of sex differentiation in mate preferences — smaller differences in more gender-equal nations. (Note: a critique of Zentner & Mitura exists: *Evolutionary Psychology* 10, 720–726, cited in the Brunel review — details not verified here.)
- **Replication-era caution** — Replicability Report for the journal *Evolutionary Psychology* (Replication Index, 2024, verified): EP has produced "both highly robust and questionable results." Robust: sex differences in sexual behaviors/attitudes. Questionable: menstrual-cycle hormonal effects on women's attitudes/behaviors (Harris 2011 had already challenged cycle-phase facial-preference findings; per Psychology in Seattle critique page). Nothing in the replication literature validates a general "women crave attention" effect because no such effect has been operationalized as a scientific construct.
- **Diagnostic bias against women** — Histrionic PD (DSM-5: "uncomfortable when not the center of attention") is diagnosed ~4x more in women, but prevalence studies find similar rates in both sexes; the diagnosis is widely regarded as gender-biased, inheriting 19th-century "hysteria" lore (Wikipedia HPD entry; Reachlink; AWIS — all secondary). Marcie Kaplan's *A Women's View of DSM-III* argued HPD pathologizes stereotypical femininity. This is exactly the kind of "proof" that turns out to be circular: the culture labels female attention-seeking as a disorder, then cites the diagnosis as evidence.
- **Freud as the historical root, not proof** — Freud's female-narcissism writings ("On Narcissism" 1914; "Femininity" lectures 1931–33; "Female Sexuality" 1931) asserted women are more narcissistic/self-regarding, but the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (verified) notes these are read as "misogynist, and perhaps Procrustean" reductions of women's experience. There is **no** first empirical "proof" that women crave attention — the claim predates empirical psychology as clinical lore.

## 4. "Women seek mate attention" vs. "women seek social attention broadly"

This is the crucial distinction, and the literature supports only the narrower version:

| Claim | Support level | What the evidence actually says |
|---|---|---|
| Women seek **mate attention** (being chosen/valued by a committed, high-investment partner) | Moderate-to-strong | Trivers 1972; Buss 1989 cross-cultural mate preferences; Buss et al. 1992 + Sagarin et al. 2012 (women more sensitive to threats to emotional bond); Sedgewick et al. 2017 (mate-context selfies). Women's jealousy targets emotional infidelity — i.e., **commitment**, not attention per se. |
| Women seek **appearance-based social approval** (being seen as attractive) | Moderate, but socioculturally amplified | Self-objectification literature (Fredrickson & Roberts 1997; Meier & Gray 2014); selfie-posting volume differences; women's intrasexual competition via beautification (Fisher 2004; Wang et al. 2021). Effect sizes are small-to-moderate and heavily mediated by cultural objectification and gender parity (Zentner & Mitura 2012; Eagly & Wood 1999). |
| Women crave **social attention broadly** (attention from anyone, for its own sake) | **Weak / unsupported** | No validated construct or EP theory makes this claim. Sociometer theory (Leary 1995) says both sexes monitor acceptance. Men show equal-or-higher status/power goals (HBS 16-030), stronger narcissism–selfie links (Sorokowski 2015), and greater status-comparison sensitivity (Ratliff & Oishi 2013). Rejection sensitivity does not differ by sex on average (Downey & Feldman 1996). Social-pain responses to ostracism are not cleanly female-specific (Onoda et al. 2010; Lamm et al. 2011). |

**The dating-profile reading:** "Be nice to me and give me attention" is better interpreted through the sociometer/mate-valuation lens — the profile writer wants reassurance of her **mate value** and **relational security** (consistent with the mate-attention and jealousy literature) — not as evidence of a general biological craving for social attention. That interpretation is a reasonable psychological reading, not an established scientific finding.

---

## 5. Bottom-line verdict

**The claim, as stated, does not hold up scientifically.** It is a folk-psychology compression of several narrower findings:

1. **Supported (narrowly):** Women are choosier in mate choice (Trivers 1972; Buss 1989); women's jealousy targets threats to emotional commitment (Buss et al. 1992; Sagarin et al. 2012); women engage in more appearance-based self-presentation and self-objectification (Sedgewick 2017; Meier & Gray 2014; objectification theory) — though these last effects are substantially sociocultural and shrink with gender equality (Eagly & Wood 1999; Zentner & Mitura 2012).

2. **Not supported (broadly):** "Women are programmed to crave attention" in general. Men seek status/power attention at least as much (HBS 16-030; Sorokowski 2015; Ratliff & Oishi 2013). Average sex differences across psychology are small (Hyde 2005; Zell et al. 2014). Ostracism/social-pain sensitivity is not cleanly sex-differentiated (Onoda 2010; Lamm 2011). The clinical "proof" (hysteria, histrionic PD) is now recognized as gender-biased, not evidence.

3. **"First record of proof":** There is none, because the claim was never operationalized as a scientific hypothesis. Its historical lineage runs through Freud's female-narcissism writings (1914–1933) and 19th-century hysteria lore — both critiqued as non-empirical and misogynistic — not through any empirical demonstration. The earliest modern empirical anchors are the cross-cultural mate-preference studies (Buss 1989) and, much later, the social-media/selfie literature (2007–2017), neither of which tests "craving attention" as such.

**Honest phrasing for Jordan:** "Women, on average, pay more attention to being valued by a mate and to how their appearance is evaluated — and men, on average, pay more attention to status and dominance. Both sexes are sensitive to social evaluation. The sizeable claim that women are 'programmed to crave attention' overstates a mating-psychology finding and ignores the evidence that men crave recognition at least as much, just in a different currency."
