# Gender Differences in Online Dating Profiles: Empirical Evidence Review

Research compiled for evaluating the claim: "dating profiles prove women crave attention — women literally list 'be nice to me and give me attention.'"

All claims below cite sources registered in the citation ledger. "Industry survey" = company-commissioned (Hinge/Match), not peer-reviewed. Base-rate evidence for women explicitly writing "give me attention" in bios was **not found** in published content analyses — that gap is itself a finding.

---

## 1. Gender differences in dating profile text and photos

### What studies actually measured

**Profile text (word-level):**
- Nagarajan & Hearst (2009) analyzed free-text portions of 500 male + 500 female Yahoo Personals profiles (ages 18–60, opposite-sex seekers) with LIWC. Finding: **overall similarity** in word-category usage between sexes; men used slightly more tentative words (2.6% vs 2.2% of words); affect/positive-emotion/sexual words appeared in both sexes' clusters; "self expression tends towards attempting homophily in online dating profiles." Authors conclude gender contrasts in word *types* are modest.[3]
- Abramova, Baumgartner, Krasnova & Buxmann (2016), HICSS systematic literature review of gender differences in online dating: typical pattern is men emphasizing physical attractiveness, women emphasizing socio-economic attributes; women more likely to misrepresent physical attractiveness, men more likely marital status/interests.[33]
- Lance (1998), content analysis of 1,433 heterosexual personal ads: males emphasized personality, good looks, slimness, professional-college degree, nonsmoking; women emphasized personality and status-related/commitment qualities (abstract partial in retrieval).[32] Classic personal-ad exchange pattern (Harrison & Saeed 1977, as summarized by Stack 1996 and Montini 1990): men more likely to *offer* financial security and *seek* attractiveness; women the reverse.[32]
- Toma & Hancock (2010), Communication Research, n=69 online daters with lab photos rated by independent judges: less attractive daters more likely to enhance photos and lie about height/weight/age; deception did not extend to income/occupation. Reviewing personal-ad literature within the paper: women drew attention to physical attractiveness and body shape; "this desire [to convey attractiveness] is particularly salient for women." Deception patterns: men lie more about height/income/interests; women about weight/age (Toma, Hancock & Ellison 2008; Concordia Journal student replication, n=14 convenience sample).[2][14]

**Profile photos (visual self-presentation):**
- Solovyeva & Logunova (Moscow Tinder case study, 400 profiles ages 20–40): men wrote longer text (7.1 vs 5.45 lines) and uploaded more photos (4.89 vs 3.47); women used more emoji (38% vs 24%); men communicated more about themselves and what they want.[6]
- Lee & Chen (2026, IAFOR, Taiwan Tinder, 200 profiles): women significantly more likely to use sexualized imagery and close-up high-angle selfies (interpreted as emotional accessibility/visual intimacy); men emphasized body parts with medium/low-angle photos (traditional masculine ideals: dominance/height); photo count and emoji use did not differ significantly.[21]
- Asadchy, Karjus, Mukhina & Schich (2024), Humanities and Social Sciences Communications: machine learning on 10,680 Tinder profile images (Estonia); gendered image-genre maps differ between men and women (abstract-level finding; genre specifics behind paywall).[20]
- Visser (2022, Leiden MSc thesis): Dutch Tinder/Bumble/Lexa bios vs 1947–1985 personal ads — personality self-description increased over time; appearance description more frequent in personal ads than in bios (photos now carry looks); intentions differ per app.[31]

**Grey literature (non-peer-reviewed, treat as signal only):**
- Black Tux blog bio analysis: women more likely to mention zodiac signs, tattoos, hair color, and emojis (40.2% vs 34.4% of men); men more likely to mention pets, outdoors, Instagram/Snapchat handles, cuddling, religion, and profanity.[34]

### Big platform data analyses (OkCupid, Tinder)

- Rudder / OkTrends, "Your Looks and Your Inbox" (2009): women on OkCupid rated 80% of men as worse-looking than medium; two-thirds of male messages went to the best-looking third of women; most attractive men got ~11× the messages of the lowest-rated, medium-rated ~4×; "it's your picture that matters most."[17]
- Rudder, "We Experiment On Human Beings!" (2014): users' "looks" and "personality" ratings were within a half point of each other for 92% of the sample after 25 votes; hiding profile text changed impressions minimally — "the text is less than 10% of what people think of you"; on Love Is Blind Day (photos removed), blind-started conversations collapsed once photos returned — "people are exactly as shallow as their technology allows them to be"; blind-date app users reported good dates regardless of partner looks, yet rated the same partners harshly online.[4]
- Bruch & Newman (2018), Science Advances, messaging data from a free dating site in 4 US cities: pronounced desirability hierarchies consistent across cities; **both** men and women pursue partners ~25% more desirable than themselves; response probability drops with desirability gap; women's desirability declines from 18 to 60 while men's peaks ~50; Asian women and white men most desirable; postgraduate education associated with *decreased* desirability for women (age-controlled); the most messaged individual was a 30-year-old woman in New York (1,504 messages in a month).[42]
- Hitsch, Hortaçsu & Ariely (2010), Quantitative Marketing and Economics: revealed-preference estimation from an online dating service — no evidence of strategic shading of stated preferences; women show stronger preference than men for income over physical attributes in actual contact decisions; strong same-race preferences; for men but not women, revealed race preferences matched stated ones.[37][38]
- Tyson et al. (2016), "A First Look at User Activity on Tinder" (14 curated profiles + survey): women attain large numbers of matches rapidly, men slowly accumulate; ~86% of matches received by male profiles came from other men (gay men far more active in liking than heterosexual women); men liked a large share of viewed women but matched at ~0.6% vs women's ~10%; 21% of women initiated conversation (avg 122-char openers) vs 7% of men (12-char openers); 63% of men's messages arrived within 5 minutes of matching vs 18% of women (median delays 2 min vs 38 min); "a little effort in grooming profiles, especially for male users, goes a long way in attracting attention."[26][41]

### What modern surveys say women want in partners (industry data)

- Hinge Labs survey (Aug 2025, 6,000+ global respondents): 72% of women care more about a partner's effort in building a relationship than about a higher income; women's top "high-value partner" traits: **emotional availability (35%), acknowledgement and respect for emotional needs (25%), consistent communication (22%)**; men's guesses of what women want: body attractiveness (24%), facial attractiveness (21%), financial wealth (20%) — a large perception gap; men's own top traits in a partner: willing to build the relationship (24%), kind (22%), loyal (22%).[24]
- Hinge 2025 Gen Z D.A.T.E. report: 84% of Gen Z Hinge daters are seeking ways to build emotional intimacy; 48% of Gen Z men hold back from emotional intimacy for fear of seeming "too much"; 43% of Gen Z women wait for the other person to initiate deep conversations; 52% of Hinge daters have felt ashamed after being emotionally vulnerable.[51]
- Match "Singles in America" 2021 (5,000+ US singles ages 18–98): emotional maturity tops the list of desired partner qualities; physical attractiveness as "important" fell from 90% (2020) to 78% (2021); good communicator 84%, open-minded 83%; 62% seek meaningful committed relationships, only 11% casual dating; **70% of single men vs 60% of single women want a relationship in the next year** — a counterexample to "women are the commitment-cravers."[25]

## 2. Do women explicitly list wanting attention, compliments, emotional effort, or affection?

**Direct answer: no published content analysis was found that quantifies how often women's dating profiles explicitly say "be nice to me," "give me attention," "compliment me," etc.** Multiple search angles (academic content analyses, profile-text NLP studies, app surveys) turned up no base-rate frequency data for that specific phrasing. The claim's apparent ubiquity comes from anecdotal browsing, not measured prevalence — and profile text is demonstrably a low-weight component of outcomes anyway (OkCupid: text <10% of impressions).[4]

What *is* measured, and how close it gets:

- **Validation/compliments as a Tinder motive:** Sumter, Vandenbosch & Ligtenberg (2017), Telematics and Informatics — Dutch emerging adults 18–30, n=163 current/former Tinder users. Six motivations identified: Love, Casual Sex, Ease of Communication, Self-Worth Validation, Thrill of Excitement, Trendiness. The Self-Worth Validation factor includes items "to improve my self-esteem," "to feel better about myself," "to feel attractive," "to get compliments about my appearance" (item mean 2.23 on a 1–5 scale). Crucially: **Self-Worth Validation did not differ significantly by gender** (women M=2.16, men M=2.29); men scored significantly higher on Casual Sex (2.33 vs 1.42, p<.001), Ease of Communication, and Thrill. Love motivation was *stronger* than Casual Sex overall.[43]
- Ranzini & Lutz (2017), Mobile Media & Communication — Tinder survey: women used Tinder more for friendship and self-validation; men more for hooking up/sex, traveling, and relationship seeking. This is the strongest published support for "women seek validation/attention via dating apps," but note relationship-seeking was *higher in men* in the same study.[27]
- Timmermans & De Caluwé (2017), Computers in Human Behavior — 58-item Tinder Motives Scale, 13 motives including seeking social validation; validation-seeking is documented for both sexes.[46]
- Lefevre (2017, as summarized by SexLab): interviews with 21 Tinder users — all but two described entertainment/ego-boost use; compliments and match-feedback serve as positive reinforcement for desirability. Tiny sample, both genders.[8]
- Morlat, Limniou & Alison (2025/2026), Frontiers in Psychology, n=305 ages 18–30: men more often casual-sex motivated, women more often serious-relationship motivated (p<0.001); **no significant self-esteem differences across high/medium/low Tinder use groups** (p=0.92) — a counterexample to "app attention feeds women's self-esteem."[19]
- Hinge 2025 (industry): what women say they want from partners is emotional availability, respect for emotional needs, and consistent communication — partner-directed emotional effort, which is different from "attention for its own sake." 72% value effort over income.[24]

**Bottom line for section 2:** The measured construct closest to Jordan's claim is (a) self-validation motives in app-use surveys — real but **not female-specific** in the best-known study (Sumter 2017) — and (b) women's stated partner preferences for emotional availability/effort (Hinge 2025) — which is wanting a partner's attention, not evidence that women seek attention as a general psychological crave. The literal claim ("women list 'give me attention'") has no published frequency data behind it.

## 3. Related behavioral evidence: social comparison, selfies, surveillance, counterexamples

### Selfie posting
- Sorokowska et al. (2016), Computers in Human Behavior, n=748 (355 women, 393 men): prior literature shows a consistent gender difference in selfie-posting frequency favoring women; but in this study, histrionic personality (self-promotion) predicted selfie counts **only in men**; narcissism/psychopathy predict selfies especially in men (Fox & Rooney 2015; Sorokowski et al. 2015, cited therein).[22]
- Current Psychology (2025), adolescents: selfie posting predicts self-objectification, and the link was **stronger for male adolescents than females** — contrary to the female-centric assumption.[47]

### Social comparison on social media
- Fardouly & Vartanian (2016), Current Opinion in Psychology (review): social media use — particularly Facebook — is associated with body image concerns in young **women and men**; appearance comparisons mediate the relationship; experimental evidence is less consistent than correlational.[29]
- PMC study (2025) with balanced gender representation: social-media use, social comparison, and self-esteem associations **did not differ significantly between men and women** — explicitly contradicting earlier female-vulnerability findings; authors attribute the shift to rising male engagement with image-based platforms.[30]
- OxJournal overview: gender differences in social comparison on social media are **inconclusive**; a University of Alberta survey (B2Press 2022, secondary source, weak) reported ~90% of women vs ~60% of men say they compare themselves on social platforms.[15]

### Social surveillance / monitoring
- Muise, Christofides & Desmarais (2009) and Muise et al. (2014), Personal Relationships: in response to jealousy, **women are more likely than men to monitor a partner's Facebook activity** ("creeping" vs. information-seeking). Gendered surveillance behavior is real — but it is partner-monitoring under jealousy, not self-promotional attention-seeking.[28]

### Platform usage patterns
- Pew Research (Jan 2025, US adults): women more likely than men to keep in touch with close friends by phone/text/social media (10+ point gaps), though among under-30s the gender gap vanishes; men slightly more likely to see friends in person.[40] Pew (Nov 2025, n=5,022): women use Instagram/Facebook/TikTok more; men use X/Reddit more.[50]
- Herring & Kapidzic (2015), encyclopedia chapter on teens, gender, and social-media self-presentation: girls post more appearance-focused/"cute" pictures and more friends; boys orient toward technology, sports, humor, and personal information. Notably, on a **teenage dating site**, women made sexual references significantly more often than men — a context reversal.[49]

### Counterexamples: men and gay men seek attention/visibility too
- **Men and self-validation:** Sumter 2017 — Self-Worth Validation motive not gender-differentiated (men ns-slightly *higher*).[43] Selfie-narcissism/histrionic links are stronger in men than women in several studies.[22][47] Match 2021 — more single men (70%) than women (60%) want a relationship in the next year.[25] Hinge Gen Z 2025 — 48% of Gen Z men hold back from emotional intimacy; men want emotional connection but fear the vulnerability.[51]
- **Gay male dating culture:** Fan et al. (2023), Computational Communication Research — computer-vision analysis of profile photos on Finka (Chinese gay dating app): appealing faces and body disclosure drive popularity; confirms "the gay male beauty myth" in online dating; appearance-driven attention competition among men.[11] Blackwell, Birnholtz & Abbott (2015) on Grindr: "seeing and being seen" — visibility, co-situation, and impression formation are central dynamics; men actively negotiate being seen/looked at.[48] Tyson et al. (2016): gay men were far more active in sending likes than heterosexual women.[26]
- **Attention received ≠ attention wanted:** Pew 2023 — ~1 in 5 women who used dating sites in the past year *often* felt overwhelmed by message volume; women ~3× as likely as men to often feel overwhelmed; men more likely to feel insecure about *lack* of messages; harassment far higher for women under 50 (56% received unwanted sexually explicit messages/images vs 26% of men; 43% experienced continued unwanted contact).[1][39] The behavioral record shows women receiving far more attention than they often want — being flooded is not the same as craving.

## 4. What the evidence proves — and what it doesn't

### What IS well-supported
1. **A replicated sex difference in stated mate preferences:** women weight earning capacity/status more, men weight physical attractiveness more — Buss (1989, 37 cultures, 10,047 participants; women valued good financial prospects more in 36/37 cultures); Abramova et al. (2016 review); Hitsch et al. (2010, revealed behavior in real messaging); Zhang et al. (2019, replication, n=3,073 across 36 countries — found the difference but little evidence it shrinks with national gender equality).[45][33][38][7]
2. **Women's photos and self-presentation on apps emphasize appearance more than the "women care about personality" folk theory suggests** (Toma & Hancock 2010; Lee & Chen 2026; personal-ad literature reviewed therein).[2][21]
3. **Women receive vastly more messages/matches than men on hetero dating markets**, and that attention is heavily concentrated among the most attractive (Tyson 2016; Bruch & Newman 2018; OkCupid 2009; Pew 2023).[26][42][17][39]
4. **Modern surveys show women naming emotional availability, effort, and communication as top partner wants** — Hinge 2025 (35%/25%/22%), Match 2021 (emotional maturity #1).[24][25]
5. **Some women use dating apps partly for self-validation/friendship** — Ranzini & Lutz 2017; Sumter et al. 2017 (modest validation means, ~2.2/5).[27][43]

### What is NOT proven
1. **No base-rate evidence for the literal claim.** No published content analysis found measuring how common it is for women to write "be nice to me / give me attention / compliment me" in bios. Anecdote ≠ prevalence; salient examples are selection-biased by definition.
2. **Stated ≠ revealed.** Eastwick & Finkel (2008, JPSP speed-dating): classic stated preferences (men=looks, women=earnings) did **not** predict live attraction; physical attractiveness predicted interest about equally for both sexes in vivo; correlations between stated preferences and actual choices ranged .00–.17; "individuals seem to lack accurate introspective access to the preferences" that guide live attraction.[36] OkCupid's own experiments: text contributes <10% of impressions; "looks" and "personality" ratings nearly identical (92% within half a point); people are "as shallow as their technology allows them to be."[4] Whatever women *write* reveals little about what drives matching.
3. **Selection bias everywhere.** Dating-app users are not the general population; profile-writers are not representative of app users; hetero-app gender ratios skew male (OkCupid ~60/40 per public data analyses); women's bios are strategic impression-management (Goffman; Ellison, Heino & Gibbs 2006 — "show, not tell"), and misrepresentation is gender-patterned (men: assets/interests; women: weight/age), so texts are not transparent windows into desires.[2][14][33]
4. **Attractiveness confounds the attention story.** Toma & Hancock (2010): less attractive daters misrepresent appearance more — profile content tracks market position, not just psychology. Rudder (2009): women rated 80% of men below medium yet messaged across the spectrum anyway. Bruch & Newman (2018): messages received correlate with desirability rank, not profile text. "Women get attention" is largely a statement about *who receives messages*, driven by attractiveness hierarchies and male swipe behavior (men swipe right on a large share of women; 35%+ mass-right-swipe per Tyson et al.'s analysis), not proof of female attention-craving.[2][17][42][26]
5. **Receiving attention ≠ wanting attention.** Pew 2023: women are ~3× as likely as men to feel *overwhelmed* by messages; harassment rates are far higher for women under 50 (56% unwanted explicit messages). The same volume Jordan reads as "craving" is documented by its recipients as burden and risk.[1][39]
6. **The validation motive is not female-specific.** Sumter et al. (2017): Self-Worth Validation ns by gender (men ns-slightly higher). Selfie–narcissism/histrionic links are stronger in men. Gay male apps are appearance/visibility-driven (Fan 2023; Blackwell 2015; Tyson 2016). Match 2021: more men than women want relationships soon.[43][22][11][48][26][25]
7. **Industry surveys (Hinge/Match) are useful signal, not definitive evidence** — commissioning party, opaque sampling, not population-representative; use for direction, not point estimates.
8. **The strongest true kernel in Jordan's claim:** women's *stated partner preferences* in modern surveys emphasize emotional availability, effort, and communication (Hinge 2025: emotional availability 35%, respect for emotional needs 25%, consistent communication 22%), and men substantially misjudge this (men guess women prioritize looks/wealth). That is evidence that women *ask for* emotional attention from partners — not that women *crave attention* as a gendered psychological trait, and not that profiles "prove" it.[24]

---

## Bottom line

The empirical record supports a narrower, less dramatic claim than "women crave attention": (1) women's stated mate preferences emphasize status/earnings and — in modern surveys — emotional availability/effort more than men's stated preferences do; (2) women receive far more attention on dating apps than men, concentrated among the most attractive; (3) self-validation motives exist in app use but are not significantly gendered in the flagship study (Sumter 2017); (4) profile text itself barely drives outcomes (OkCupid: <10% of impressions), stated preferences poorly predict live attraction (Eastwick & Finkel 2008), and the literal "women list 'give me attention'" phrasing has no measured base rate in any content analysis found. The behavioral data cut the other way as often as not: the attention women receive is frequently documented as overwhelming or harassing (Pew 2023), and counterexamples abound (men's validation-seeking, gay male appearance economies, men's higher stated relationship-seeking in Match 2021). Dating profiles can illustrate that some women ask for emotional effort from partners; they cannot "prove" a gendered craving for attention.
